Cash is out, now what?

In 2007 the Reserve Bank asked Australians to record every payment they made for a week. Cash made up 69% of them. By 2025 it was 15%. This page goes through what replaced it: debit cards, tap-and-go, phone payments, the networks behind the tap, and newer options like PayID. The second half looks at where people can still get cash. [1, 2, 4]

Where it started

Cash was most payments in 2007

The survey only splits the country into capital cities and everywhere else, and both started at about 69%. The regions kept using cash for longer. The gap was biggest in 2016, at 34% of payments in the capitals and 44% everywhere else, and by 2022 it was under three points. Where people lived changed how fast they stopped using cash, but not whether they did. [1, 2]

Layer 1 · Debit cards

Debit cards replaced cash, not credit cards

People were spending money already in their account, on terminals shops already had. [1, 4]

Layer 2 · Tap and go

Tapping took over small purchases

Small purchases were the last place cash held on. [2]

Layer 3 · Phone wallets

Younger people pay with their phones

Older people are taking it up more slowly, so the gap between them keeps growing. [1, 2]

Layer 4 · The networks behind the tap

Every tap costs the shop something

Each card payment goes through a network, and the network sets the fee the shop pays. [2, 3]

Layer 5 · Newer ways to pay

Most new ways to pay are known but not used

PayID is the exception. [1]

That covers how Australians pay. The rest of the page is about where they can still get cash. Cash use stopped falling in 2025, at about 15% of payments, but the places to get it kept closing. Between June 2017 and June 2025 Australia lost 44% of its bank branches and 63% of its bank-owned ATMs. [1, 4]

Layer 6 · Places to get cash

Places to get cash are closing

Using less cash is a choice. Losing your local bank branch isn't. [4]

Why cash stays

Cash still works when the network doesn't

In 2026 the government made it law that shops selling essentials have to accept cash. [8, 9]

Melbourne

The same thing is happening in Melbourne

Branches have closed across the city, and some suburbs now have almost nowhere to get cash. [4]

What it adds up to

Across seven surveys from 2007 to 2025, cash fell from 69% of payments to 15%. Debit cards replaced it first, then tapping a card or phone, and this happened at about the same rate in cities and regions. Access to cash didn't. Australia closed 44% of its bank branches in eight years, outside the major cities post offices are now 47–69% of face-to-face banking points, and Greater Melbourne went from 848 branches to 430. The 2026 law says shops selling essentials have to accept cash. Whether people can still get cash depends on where they live. [1, 2, 4, 8]

Sources and notes

About this page

Made for FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 at Monash University, Semester 2 2026. It's written for Australian adults with no finance background. It shows how quickly the country moved away from cash, who moved first, and where getting cash is getting harder. Every chart and map is Vega-Lite or Vega; the JSON for each is in the specs/ folder of the repository.

What's plotted

The two opening charts use the Reserve Bank's published cash shares by number of payments, 2007–2025. Non-cash is calculated as 100 minus cash. The percentages are rounded survey estimates, not a count of all Australian transactions.

The Reserve Bank's Table 1 excludes payments over $9,999, automatic payments, transfers to family and friends, and transport cards. Online payments are included. Lines connect survey waves, not annual observations.

Scott Kim & Michael Reschke, Consumer Payment Behaviour in Australia. Reserve Bank of Australia Bulletin, 28 May 2026, Table 1. Underlying data workbook.

What isn't observed

No public dataset gives payment mix below the capital-city/regional split, so every map of smaller areas shows access to cash (branches, Bank@Post outlets, ATMs and EFTPOS terminals from APRA) or the Australian Digital Inclusion Index, not how people in those places pay. Nothing on this page is modelled or imputed; every figure is a published or summed value from the sources listed.

How the data was prepared

Reserve Bank survey tables were used as published. APRA point records were summed by state, remoteness area, region and suburb, and divided by the populations APRA attaches to each suburb. ABS boundaries were simplified in mapshaper to keep each map under 700 KB. The tidy CSV files the charts load are in the data/ folder of the repository.
